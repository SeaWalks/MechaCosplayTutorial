# Materials

This page is a quick overview of the materials I use in my building my armor cosplays. 
## EVA Foam

Disclaimer, I'm no chemist or expert. The the stuff I use, XPLE (Cross linked poly ethylene) foam seems to be used interchangeably with EVA foam in some places. Different cosplay focused websites will sell their own branded foam, which blend different densities/hardness. I don't recommend these because 1) The sheets are tend to be too small for our purposes and 2) They are expensive. If you want to splurge, go for it- I hear great things, and the cosplayers they sponsor are absolute monsters. But for our purposes, our big cheap rolls work excellent. 

- Types of EVA Foam: Here is the TLDR: you are looking for large rolls (48”x70”) of double sided (so clean on both sides) foam. That way you don’t have the issue of having a pattern too big for your foam. Without a big deep dive, you generally want L400/4LB eva foam. 

- For smaller parts, Hobby Lobby and Michaels have rolls of foam that can be decently priced- anything around 1$ /sqft is what you're looking for. However, due to the width of these rolls (2 ft), it can be a little small for making larger parts like shields. 

> Recommendation: The best place I have found to purchase large EVA Foam rolls online is [here](https://www.foambymail.com/product/cross-linked-polyethylene-foam-4lb.html). The 4lb works great as an all rounder, 2LB is floppier and lighter, the 6LB is stiffer and heavier. Grab enough to get free shipping for even better value- this even beats out FoamMart for us California locals. 

# Foam vs 3D printing: 

This guide will be written with EVA foam in mind for simple reason that I don't have a ton of experience with FDM 3D printing. It is important to note that there are massive advances in filament that are huge for the cosplay community. TPU/Foaming TPU specifically are massive advances that mitigate a lot of the traditional downsides of 3D printing. There are two reasons that I would recommend using EVA foam over traditional filaments (PETG, PLA) for Gundam builds. 

- Weight- EVA foam parts can be absurdly light- my GQux beam rifle was less than 1Lb. A 3d printed version would most likely be many times that. Every shaved pound will make your experience much much more enjoyable, and could make the difference between a miserable trip back to the car to desuit in the middle of the day versus comfortably enjoying the whole day in cosplay.

Weight also comes into play when it comes to parts supporting themselves. A large printed backpack with wings would be much more likely to sag and crack under its own weight as opposed to something made with foam, which can be both stable and light, especially if combined with other rigid material like corrugated cardboard/plastic. If you’re familiar with the [square cube law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square%E2%80%93cube_law), you’ll know what I’m talking about. 
    
- Flexibility- When you push your suits as hard as I do, bumping into things (or things bumping into you) is an inevitability. Having parts flex instead of snap or shatter is invaluable, particularly around the feet.


## Adhesives
A good glue keeps your cosplay in one piece. Below is a list of all the common adhesives I use in cosplay, along with recommendations on what to buy. 
 - Contact Cement: This is the #1 adhesive for EVA Foam. Incredibly strong, flexible bond. When done properly, the foam itself will tear before the glue joint. Two options here: Barge Cement, and Weldwood Contact cement (In the red can). In my experience, they perform the same, with weldwood being a good bit cheaper. The most cost efficient route would be to buy a gallon of the weldwood from walmart and pour it out as needed into smaller, airtight squeeze bottles. I typically buy a pint at the time, and it lasts me quite a while, with an average build taking around 3 jars of glue. Two warnings.
    - It is toxic, so use it in a well ventilated area ideally with a respirator. 
    - It gets gummier and harder to work with as it oxidizes so try to keep contact with the air to a minimum.
> Recommendation: Weldwood Contact Cement, in the red can. Recommend buying in Pints or Quarts- the smaller sizes lose too much value, and the gallons sizes force you to decant glue into smaller vessels which could be a pain. 
- Hot Glue: The cosplayer’s best friend. If you can use contact cement, use cement instead- it is much stronger. Hot glue is great for reinforcing seams from the back, gluing non-foam things to foam, or really sticking anything to any other thing quickly and easily. It comes in two sizes- mini and regular. For my bet, the full size is generally the better option here. 
> Recommendation: The cheapest, biggest box of the stuff you can find on Amazon. 
- Two part Epoxy: The strongest adhesive in our arsenal. Rarely used, but when you need it nothing else will do. A pain to work with, but if you need to glue mission critical stuff (anything involving shoes) this is the way. I’ll usually keep a tube of this stuff in the toolbox and treat it as a one time use. 
> Recommendation: Whatever you find at the store- these all more or less seem to work the same in my experience. 
- E6000: A step behind in strength compared to a two part epoxy, but a good deal more flexible. Great for locking down straps (although if you know how to sew, that would probably be better). More of a nice to have than an essential. 
> Recommendation: Medium sized tube on Amazon should last you forever.
- Superglue: Great for quick fixes at the con, but during construction I rarely use this stuff. Great for gluing on small greeblies, but wouldn’t count on it holding anything stronger- just do the right thing and use contact cement instead. 
> Recommendation: A pack of a bunch of tubes/nozzles; if a tube goes bad, you’ll have a lot, lot more. 
TLDR: Grab a pint of Weldwood contact cement, a box of hot glue sticks, and a pack of super glue tubes and you'll be set 99% of the time.
